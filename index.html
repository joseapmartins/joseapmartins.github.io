Beyond the Kuhn Segment: Conformational Substructures and Relaxation Dynamics in Flexible Chains

What is a statistical segment? Polymer chains are described in terms of statistical segments, and models — from the random coil to reptation theory — are built upon them. The random coil picture treats the chain as a succession of identical, uncorrelated segments, each behaving as a Gaussian (entropic) spring.

I show that the minimal statistical segment is the Kuhn segment, and that the monomer-based statistical segment b used by Doi & Edwards and others is not statistical at local length scales — its length falls below the persistence length. The minimal entropic spring with mild Gaussianity requires at least two Kuhn segments; the full Gaussian regime requires blocks spanning at least ten.

Based on a probabilistic classification of backbone conformations, Kuhn segments are not all identical, as assumed by all existing theories. They fall into three distinct populations: Aligned Chain Segments (ACS, red) — locally extended, slow-relaxing, interacting with neighbouring ACS to form Local Ordered Regions (LOR) in the melt, present even in atactic polymers; Random Conformational Sequences (RCS, blue) — coiled segments connecting two ACS, with greater conformational freedom; and Chain Ends (CE, orange), which behave similarly to RCS but are bounded by a single ACS.

Because ACS are aligned and interact with neighbouring segments, they relax orientationally an order of magnitude more slowly than RCS and CE, with stretched-exponential decay (β ≈ 0.5). This heterogeneity provides a direct molecular interpretation of the stretched-exponential relaxation universally observed in polymer melts, where β encodes the cooperativity of conformational rearrangements at the Kuhn scale.

About

I've graduated in Physics (five-year course) at the University of Lisbon. I started my scientific career in a State Laboratory, working with a Van de Graaff generator doing Rutherford backscattering experiments. I entered the polymer field after obtaining a degree in Materials Science (IST — Technical University of Lisbon) and a PhD in Polymer Science and Engineering (University of Minho). Both my Master's and PhD were on the modelling of polymer crystallization.

After my PhD, I invented and built, with other colleagues, a shear-DTA instrument and started a set of experiments to understand crystallization after shear deformations and the origin of the melt memory effect. Surprisingly, both amorphous and semicrystalline polymers respond similarly to shear deformations applied to the molten state. This led me to study shear-induced structures and the effect of shear deformations and melt temperature on these structures.

Around 2012, I turned to molecular dynamics simulations without knowing anything about the subject. I received the assistance of Nuno Micaelo, who helped me set up the first systems and taught me the basics. Later, I developed codes to read trajectory files and analyse them on a Kuhn-segment basis. Slowly — over about ten years — this work developed into a detailed analysis of melt morphology for entangled and unentangled systems at different temperatures, their crystallization, and their response to shear deformations.

So far, I've studied flexible and semiflexible polymer chains, amorphous polymers, and polymers with different tacticities. Almost all these results are still unpublished, mainly due to lack of time and to difficulties at the institutional level.

Research themes

What is an entanglement?

They are described as topological constraints or by "slip-links". Both assume a binary interaction. In my view, topological constraints result from hard-core repulsive interactions and cannot explain the elastic properties demonstrated by entangled polymer chains. Thus, entanglements are not topological constraints (see my work "Toward a physical definition of entanglements").

For a "slip-link" to exist, one has to consider a binary interaction between chain segments and associate to this interaction a potential energy. For this to occur, one has to look in detail at the morphology of the chain at the atomic scale (see section IV.C.4. Relation to entanglements in "Beyond the Kuhn Segment: Conformational Substructures and Relaxation Dynamics in Flexible Chains"). Here you find an important result: nRCS kBT ≈ 1.67 MPa for polyethylene. This is not an accidental result.

Why am I addressing this problem, and why did I feel the need to question the current understanding of entanglements? While studying the origin of the melt memory effect in polymer crystallization (Macromolecules 39, 7626–7634, 2006) I found that saturation of crystallization under shear occurs when the polymer melt reaches a specific state — the steady state. At this state, around 1/3 of the initial entanglements are lost and precursor structures for crystallization are created. These structures persist in melts for times much longer than the reptation time. This means that in polymer melts there is still an undefined relaxation time that is linked to these precursor structures and to the steady state. Further, all the above features are independent of polymer tacticities — they exist also in atactic polymer melts.

Hence the key question raised in the Macromolecules 39, 7626–7634, 2006 paper: how can the precursor structures — which are known from experimental results to be short-range ordered structures with chain segments oriented in the flow direction — coexist in a "disentangled" melt at the steady state of shear, which has both elongational and rotational components? Why is a melt at steady state "disentangled"?

A possible explanation requires the mosaic model: a relaxed polymer melt with chains above the critical mass is composed of short-range ordered regions where the position of the chain's statistical segments resembles that existing in the unit cell of the crystalline polymer. These regions are composed of the ACS segments. The RCS segments between two ACS are the entropy reservoirs — they are responsible for the entropic elasticity. When flow is applied, the existing short-range ordered regions aggregate into larger regions, their overall number in the melt decreasing. The entanglement loss results only from the aggregation of the short-range order into larger regions oriented with the flow direction. Because the segments in these regions are longer and more aligned with the flow, they persist for a longer time. Hence the longest relaxation time in polymer melts is the isotropization time of these ordered regions in the melt at the steady state.

Melt memory & crystallization

Kinetics under quiescent and flow conditions. In start-up shear experiments, the viscosity of both semicrystalline and amorphous polymers saturates at steady state, corresponding to a loss of about one third of the initial entanglements. This loss, together with chain orientation, is responsible for the acceleration of crystallization kinetics in semicrystalline polymers.

Short-range order

I have identified the short-range order that explains the experimentally demonstrated "solid-like" behaviour in polymer melts. In these melts, as in any condensed phase, there are density fluctuations. In polymers, they occur when aligned chain segments interact with each other. I have demonstrated that the spatial arrangement of chain segments in these regions is the same as that in the crystalline unit cell — a fluid containing a "seed" with the information on the preferred spatial arrangement of molecules in the solid state.

Instrumentation

Design and construction of a shear-DTA instrument. Rheometer and DSC calibration. Systematic analysis of temperature errors in thermal analysis and their propagation into crystallization kinetics.
